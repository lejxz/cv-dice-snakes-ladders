# Introduction

**Project Title:** Self-Verifying Real-Time Dice Recognition Using Blob Detection and Morphology for a Computer Vision-Driven Snakes and Ladders Game

Traditional tabletop board games such as Snakes and Ladders owe much of their social engagement and enjoyment to the tactile ritual of rolling a physical die, yet they depend entirely on players to visually read outcomes, track positions, and enforce rules manually. Digital adaptations automate board state, ladder climbs, snake slides, and turn transitions, but they replace the physical die with a software pseudo-random number generator triggered by a button click, diminishing the physical anticipation and tangible fairness that players value (Benford, Magerkurth, & Ljungstrand, 2005). Bridging these two experiences—allowing players to throw a real die on a table while a computer automatically reads the top face and advances the digital token—introduces a strict reliability requirement: in live gameplay, a single misread die face immediately corrupts the turn, potentially triggering the wrong snake or ladder and undermining player trust. Because real-world overhead video feeds contain motion blur during bouncing, specular glare, border shadows, arbitrary planar rotations, and occluding hands, the core challenge is not simply counting dots in a clean photograph, but reliably detecting when a physical roll has come to rest and verifying whether each automated reading is trustworthy before updating the game state.

Prior studies and applications attempting to link physical dice and board games with digital software have generally pursued either hardware-instrumented dice or camera-based computer vision. On the hardware side, pervasive gaming prototypes such as RFIDice embed passive radio-frequency identification (RFID) tags and metallic shielding inside custom dice so an under-table antenna array can infer the upward face (Hinske & Langheinrich, 2008), while commercial smart dice integrate internal accelerometers and wireless transmitters to broadcast roll values. On the vision side, tangible tabletop systems have shown that a standard overhead camera can turn unmodified physical game pieces into interactive digital inputs (Molla & Lepetit, 2010), and temporal inference across image sequences has been used to track physical board game moves despite transient occlusions from players' hands (Scher, Crabb, & Davis, 2008). Specifically for automated dice recognition, classical machine vision studies have combined thresholding, morphological filtering, and region clustering—such as modified unsupervised grey clustering (Huang, 2008) and structural feature analysis using circularity thresholds and relative inter-pip distances (Chen & Chung, 2011)—to segment dice and compute pip counts from overhead images, while newer hobbyist and academic projects often rely on deep learning object detectors trained on labeled dice datasets.

Despite these developments, existing solutions exhibit key limitations that make them either impractical or unreliable for an accessible, real-time hybrid Snakes and Ladders interface. Hardware-augmented dice require custom fabrication, specialized sensing tables, or battery charging, and embedded electronics can alter the weight distribution and low-cost simplicity of an ordinary die. Conversely, many classical vision-based dice readers evaluate static photographs under controlled laboratory lighting (Huang, 2008; Chen & Chung, 2011) rather than continuous live video streams, leaving them without a temporal roll-event mechanism to distinguish a bouncing or hand-held die from a settled throw. More importantly, most existing single-frame pip counters—whether classical region counters or black-box neural networks—lack explicit self-verification and a formal reject option (Chow, 1970; El-Yaniv & Wiener, 2010). Under real tabletop lighting, a stray specular highlight or edge shadow that leaves one to six detected regions is silently accepted as a valid face (for instance, misclassifying a four-pip face plus one glare artifact as a five), directly causing erroneous token movement. Meanwhile, deep learning pipelines introduce dataset labeling overhead, heavier compute requirements, and opaque failure modes that are unnecessary for recognizing a high-contrast, geometrically structured object.

To address these limitations, this project proposes a self-verifying, real-time classical computer vision system that reads a standard, unmodified 16 mm white physical die through a single top-down smartphone or USB camera and drives a digital Snakes and Ladders game entirely on CPU without deep learning. The pipeline uses dual-resolution preprocessing—downscaling the play-area region of interest for fast Otsu or adaptive thresholding (Otsu, 1979), morphological opening and closing (Haralick, Sternberg, & Zhuang, 1987), and rotated bounding-box localization—while preserving full-resolution detail to warp the detected top die face into an upright 120×120 patch. A temporal Roll Event Detection finite-state machine tracks frame-to-frame centroid displacement so recognition is triggered only after the die remains stationary for N consecutive frames, preventing premature reads during motion and latching each physical throw to a single turn. Crucially, each settled window passes through a three-layer self-verification mechanism: (1) dual-method pip counting requiring agreement between OpenCV's `SimpleBlobDetector` and connected-component analysis, (2) geometric 3×3 pip layout verification that maps detected pip centroids onto canonical Western die face templates to reject physically invalid noise or glare patterns, and (3) multi-frame majority voting with a confidence gate that flags ambiguous reads as `UNCERTAIN` and prompts a re-roll rather than committing an error. Integrated with a lightweight PyGame Snakes and Ladders board, an annotated live camera feed, and manual keyboard override controls, the system targets at least 95% pip-read accuracy on accepted settled rolls at 15 FPS or higher, evaluated across at least 100 physical rolls through ablation studies and an accuracy–rejection analysis.
