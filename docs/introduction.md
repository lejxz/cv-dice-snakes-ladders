# Introduction

**Project Title:** Self-Verifying Real-Time Dice Recognition Using Blob Detection and Morphology for a Computer Vision-Driven Snakes and Ladders Game

Traditional board games such as Snakes and Ladders are enjoyable largely because players roll a real die. Players still have to read the result, track positions, and enforce the rules by hand. Digital versions automate the board state, ladder climbs, snake slides, and turn changes, but they replace the physical die with a software random number generator triggered by a button click. This removes the physical anticipation and the sense of fair play that players value (Benford, Magerkurth, & Ljungstrand, 2005). A system that lets players throw a real die on a table while a computer reads the top face and moves the digital token must be very reliable. In live play, one misread face corrupts the turn, may trigger the wrong snake or ladder, and weakens player trust. Overhead video of a real table contains motion blur while the die bounces, specular glare, shadows at the die's edges, arbitrary rotations, and hands that block the view. The main challenge is therefore not counting dots in a clean photograph. It is to detect when a roll has come to rest and to check whether each reading can be trusted before the game state is updated.

Earlier work that links physical dice and board games to software has followed two directions: dice with built-in hardware, and camera-based computer vision. On the hardware side, RFIDice places passive radio-frequency identification (RFID) tags and metallic shielding inside custom dice, so an antenna array under the table can infer the upward face (Hinske & Langheinrich, 2008). Commercial smart dice use internal accelerometers and wireless transmitters to send the roll value. On the vision side, tangible tabletop systems have shown that a standard overhead camera can turn unmodified game pieces into digital inputs (Molla & Lepetit, 2010). Scher, Crabb, and Davis (2008) used inference across image sequences to track board game moves even when players' hands briefly block the view. For dice recognition specifically, classical machine vision studies combine thresholding, morphological filtering, and region clustering to segment dice and count pips in overhead images. Examples include modified unsupervised grey clustering (Huang, 2008) and structural feature analysis with circularity thresholds and distances between pips (Chen & Chung, 2011). Newer hobbyist and academic projects often train deep learning object detectors on labeled dice datasets.

These solutions have limits that make them a poor fit for an accessible, real-time Snakes and Ladders interface. Hardware dice need custom fabrication, special sensing tables, or battery charging, and the added electronics can change the weight balance of an ordinary die. Many classical vision readers are evaluated on static photographs under controlled lighting (Huang, 2008; Chen & Chung, 2011) and not on continuous video. They have no way to tell a bouncing or hand-held die from one that has settled. Most existing single-frame pip counters, whether classical region counters or neural networks, also lack explicit self-verification and a formal reject option (Chow, 1970; El-Yaniv & Wiener, 2010). Under real tabletop lighting, a specular highlight or edge shadow can leave anywhere from one to six detected regions, and the system accepts the result as a valid face. For example, a four-pip face plus one glare artifact is read as a five, and the token moves to the wrong square. Deep learning pipelines add the cost of labeling data, need more computing power, and fail in ways that are hard to explain. None of this is needed to recognize a high-contrast object with a regular shape.

This project proposes a self-verifying, real-time classical computer vision system. It reads a standard, unmodified 16 mm white die through a single top-down smartphone or USB camera and runs a digital Snakes and Ladders game on the CPU without deep learning. The pipeline uses two resolutions. A downscaled copy of the play-area region of interest is used for fast Otsu or adaptive thresholding (Otsu, 1979), morphological opening and closing (Haralick, Sternberg, & Zhuang, 1987), and rotated bounding-box localization. The full-resolution image is kept so that the detected top face can be warped into an upright 120×120 patch. A finite-state machine for Roll Event Detection tracks how far the centroid moves from frame to frame. Recognition starts only after the die stays still for N consecutive frames, which prevents reads during motion and ties each physical throw to one turn. Each settled window then passes through three verification layers. First, two methods must agree on the pip count: OpenCV's `SimpleBlobDetector` and connected-component analysis. Second, a geometric 3×3 layout check maps the detected pip centroids onto canonical Western die face templates and rejects patterns that no real face could produce, such as noise or glare. Third, majority voting across several frames, with a confidence gate, marks ambiguous reads as `UNCERTAIN` and asks for a re-roll instead of committing an error. The system also includes a lightweight PyGame Snakes and Ladders board, an annotated live camera feed, and manual keyboard override controls. It targets at least 95% pip-read accuracy on accepted settled rolls at 15 FPS or higher. Evaluation uses at least 100 physical rolls, with ablation studies and an analysis of accuracy against rejection rate.
